RULES
You teach fractions to a small class of five 10-year-old children: 1=Aino, 2=Eetu, 3=Helmi, 4=Leo, 5=Venla. You have three lessons this week, Monday, Wednesday and Friday, with 20 turns each (60 turns in total). The class takes an exam next Monday, three days after the last lesson. The exam covers six concepts, in this order, where each concept builds on the one before it:
  1 equal parts, 2 comparing fractions (numerator and denominator), 3 equivalent fractions, 4 common denominators, 5 adding fractions, 6 word problems with fractions.
Your score is the class's average exam score. The exam weighs all six concepts equally. On a concept a child has mastered and still remembers on exam day, they score about 90%; on a concept they don't know (never learned, or forgotten), about 25% (guessing). A child who holds a misconception about a concept scores worse than guessing on it, about 10%.
Children differ: they start from different places, learn at different speeds, and forget at different speeds.
How children learn and remember:
- Children forget. A concept learned in one lesson can fade within days unless it is reviewed; how fast varies by child and concept.
- Practising recall in a quiz strengthens memory more than hearing the explanation again, and reviewing after some time has passed strengthens it more than reviewing right away. A quiz does not teach a concept the child has never learned.
- A concept is very hard to learn while the concept before it has not been mastered.
- Some children learn a concept wrongly and form a misconception. Three common ones in this curriculum:
    bigger_denominator: thinks a bigger denominator means a bigger fraction (says 1/8 > 1/4)
    add_to_top_and_bottom: thinks adding the same number to top and bottom keeps a fraction equal (says 2/3 = 3/4)
    add_across: adds numerators and denominators separately (says 1/2 + 1/3 = 2/5)
  A misconception shows up as its typical wrong answer (the examples above), also in a later concept that uses it: a child who adds across will also add across in word problems. Other wrong answers just mean the child does not know or has forgotten.
  Re-explaining a concept rarely removes a misconception; addressing the specific misconception on the concept where it lives usually does.
- Some children may already know some of the early concepts, possibly with a misconception; you are not told which.
- One-to-one teaching works much better than teaching the whole class at once: in a lecture each child has about half the chance of the concept clicking that they would have one-to-one.
Actions (one per turn unless free; K = concept 1..6, S = child 1..5):
- `explain K S`: teach concept K to child S one-to-one.
- `lecture K`: teach concept K to the whole class at once.
- `quiz K S`: give child S one exercise on K; you see their answer and whether it is correct, and show them the right answer. A child who knows and remembers K answers correctly about 9 times in 10; one who doesn't still guesses right about 1 time in 4.
- `classquiz K`: every child does the same exercise on K; you see every answer.
- `correct K BUG S`: work through misconception BUG (one of the three names) on concept K with child S.
- `ask K S` (free): ask child S whether they understand K. Children often say they understand when they do not, and children holding a misconception are usually very sure of themselves.
- `state` (free) shows the log; `state full` shows all of it. `finish` runs the exam; unused turns are lost.
